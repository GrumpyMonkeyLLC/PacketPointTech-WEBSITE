Antivirus vs. EDR: what a small business actually needs

November 2026 · 4 minute read

If you’ve shopped for computer security lately, you’ve probably seen “EDR” everywhere. Here’s what it is, how it differs from the antivirus you already know, and how to decide what you need.

Traditional antivirus

Antivirus checks files against a list of known bad software. If something on your computer matches the list, it gets blocked or quarantined. It’s good at stopping common, well-known threats, and it’s cheap or free. Windows includes Microsoft Defender Antivirus out of the box.

The weakness is that attackers don’t always use known malware. Many attacks today use normal tools already on the computer, or brand-new code that isn’t on any list yet.

EDR (endpoint detection and response)

EDR watches what’s happening on each computer, not just which files are there. It notices suspicious behavior, like a Word document launching PowerShell or a program suddenly encrypting hundreds of files, and it can:

  • Stop the activity and isolate that computer from the network.
  • Show exactly what happened, step by step, so you know what was touched.
  • Alert someone, ideally a person who will actually look at it.

The catch: EDR is only as good as whoever watches the alerts. An EDR tool that nobody checks is not much better than antivirus.

So which do you need?

  • A few computers, no sensitive data: Microsoft Defender, kept up to date, plus MFA on email is a reasonable start.
  • Customer financial or health data, card payments, or anything regulated (PCI DSS, HIPAA): you want EDR, and someone responsible for responding to it.
  • You already use Microsoft 365 Business Premium: you may already be paying for Microsoft Defender for Business, which includes EDR. It’s often just not turned on.

Security isn’t one product

Whatever you choose, the basics matter more: MFA everywhere, updates installed, backups that actually restore, and staff who know what a scam looks like.
